How many sales is one Gumroad rating?

316 products publishing real unit sales · 450,651 units · 229 usable pairs
There is no fixed multiplier, and that is the finding. Across 229 Gumroad products that publish both a sales count and a rating count, the median paid listing sells ×25.5 its rating count — but the middle half spans ×11.7 to ×54.2, and the ratio climbs steadily with the size of the listing.

Why there is no single multiplier

The number sellers quote each other is “about thirty sales per rating”, sometimes a hundred. Neither has anything behind it. Here is what 229 products that publish both a unit-sales count and a rating count actually show.

Read the fourth column before the third. For paid products the middle half of listings sit between ×11.7 and ×54.2 — a factor of 4.6 between the 25th and 75th percentile, before counting the quarter of listings above and the quarter below. Applying one multiplier to one listing is guessing inside that range and calling it measurement.

Said the way a seller experiences it: the median paid listing converts about 3.9% of its buyers into raters. On free products it is 4.1% — people who paid nothing say less, which is the opposite of the common assumption that free products farm reviews.

The multiplier is not a constant. It rises with the size of the listing.

This is the part that makes a fixed number actively misleading, and it is visible two independent ways.

Read that second table with a correction in mind. When a listing has at least one rating, its ratio can never exceed its sales count — a product with six sales cannot show a multiplier above six. So the low bands there are censored by construction and the rise across that table is partly mechanical. The ratings-cut table above it is not censored that way, and it shows the same climb from ×22.0 to ×26.4. That is why both are printed: one of them would have been a nicer chart and a worse answer.

Across paid listings the ratio does not move with price in any direction worth acting on. Whether a buyer leaves a rating appears to be a function of how many buyers there are, not of what they paid.

Does the proxy this whole site runs on actually work?

Every other page here uses ratings to compare demand, and every one of them says so. This sample is the only place that can test it. Across the 316 products that publish a sales count, the rank correlation between ratings and units sold is 0.831 (0.784 among the ones with at least one rating).

That is a strong relationship, and it licenses exactly one use: ratings rank demand reliably, and measure it badly. If listing A has four times the ratings of listing B, A almost certainly outsells B. If you want to know by how much, the tables above are the honest answer and they are wide.

An unrated listing has not necessarily sold nothing

87 of the 316 products publishing a sales count have zero ratings. Their median is 8 units; 37 have ten or more, 8 have over a hundred, and the largest has 1,320 sales and not one rating.

They are excluded from every median above, because sales divided by zero ratings is not a number. That exclusion is also why those medians are a lower bound: the products where buyers rate least are precisely the ones the ratio cannot see.

What this sample is

1,359 Gumroad product pages fetched individually from 824 sellers. 316 of them — 23.3% — publish a unit-sales count (23.7% of paid listings, 20.7% of free ones), covering 450,651 units sold in total across 214 sellers. The ratio is measured on the 229 of those that also have at least one rating.

The category limit comes first and it is stated in full at the top of this page: the crawl behind these products has not finished and its sample is uneven, so it leans on one branch of the marketplace rather than being a cross-section of it.

Two further biases, and which way each cuts. First, displaying a sales counter is opt-in, and a seller with nothing to show is likelier to leave it off — so this is not a random draw of Gumroad products, and the listings in it are larger than typical. Second, the ratio needs a rating to exist at all, which drops the 87 zero-rating listings and biases the medians down. Neither is fixable from public data; both are stated wherever these figures appear.

Which sample, and why not the other one. These products come from the category walk, re-fetched one page at a time — the same population as the seller analysis, not the 42-search sample of 1,344 products every other guide here is built on. The two samples disagree on price by a wide margin and are published as measured. Nothing on this page is averaged with anything from the other one.
